How BridgeWatch works

Data. Every annual FHWA National Bridge Inventory file from 1992 to 2025 (34 years, all states, DC and Puerto Rico; record type 1 only). 2025 is the last year in the 1995 Coding Guide format.

One history per bridge. Several states renumbered most of their bridges in a single year. BridgeWatch links old and new IDs by padding-only changes, by an identical physical description, or by location within 50 m, and refuses links to structures built in the transition year (replacements). This cut year-to-year ID breaks from 2.2% to 1.1%.

Questions. Q1: will this bridge become Poor (lowest of deck, superstructure, substructure, culvert ≤ 4)? Q3: will an open bridge be posted for load, restricted or closed? Q4: is a bridge on a 24-month cycle low-risk enough for 48 months? Q2: with a budget, which bridges first?

Models. Gradient-boosted trees on features from each bridge's own past only (ratings, trends, age, traffic, material, design, load rating, scour, inspection interval, location). Risk cannot fall as a rating gets worse. Every claim is checked against baselines in a time-split backtest: "rating stays the same" (worst-first), time-in-condition, and a Markov chain like those used in agency asset-management tools.

Costs. FHWA 2025 bridge replacement unit costs by state and system; rehabilitation = 68% of replacement (FHWA's own assumption). Detour cost uses federal benefit-cost values for vehicle operating cost and travel time, at an assumed 40 mph.

Limits. Ratings are visual and vary between inspectors. Repairs are not recorded in the NBI and are inferred. The planner measures targeting, not whether a treatment would have prevented an event. Nothing here is an engineering assessment of a specific bridge; the owner agency's inspection and posting are authoritative.
